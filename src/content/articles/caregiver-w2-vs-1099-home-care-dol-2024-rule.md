On January 10, 2024, the Department of Labor's final rule on independent contractor classification took effect. The rule, codified at 29 C.F.R. Part 795, replaced a 2021 rule that had made it easier for businesses to classify workers as independent contractors under the Fair Labor Standards Act. For the home care industry — which employs roughly 3.4 million direct care workers according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, many of them working in arrangements that blur the line between employee and contractor — the 2024 rule has real operational and accounting consequences that most operators haven't fully worked through.

## What the DOL's 2024 rule actually says

The 2024 rule reinstated the "economic reality" standard, which the DOL has applied since the 1940s and which evaluates whether a worker is economically dependent on the employer (employee) or in business for themselves (independent contractor). The rule identifies six factors, with no single factor determinative:

1. **Opportunity for profit or loss.** Can the worker affect their earnings through their own business decisions — setting their own rates, acquiring equipment, marketing to clients directly?

2. **Investment in facilities and equipment.** Does the worker invest in tools, materials, or facilities used in the work beyond what an employee would normally supply?

3. **Degree of permanence.** Is the work relationship indefinite, continuous, or exclusive, or is it project-based with a defined end and multiple simultaneous clients?

4. **Nature and degree of control.** Does the company control when, where, and how the work is done? Does it set the schedule, define the care plan, and prohibit the worker from taking outside clients?

5. **Extent to which the work is integral.** Is the worker's service a core part of the company's business, or an ancillary or specialty function the company uses on an occasional basis?

6. **Skill and initiative.** Does the worker bring specialized skills that they use in an entrepreneurial way, or do they perform work according to the company's defined process?

For home care caregivers providing personal care, companion services, or home health aide services under agency supervision, most of these factors point toward employee status. The care plan comes from the agency (or the client's case manager, not the worker). The schedule is set by the agency's matching process. The worker typically cannot bring their own clients to the engagement or work for competitors without the agency's permission. The service — providing direct care to clients — is the core of what the agency sells.

This doesn't mean every home care agency using 1099 caregivers is automatically in violation. It means the facts need to be evaluated honestly, and the accounting needs to reflect the realistic economic relationship.

## The state-law layer on top of the federal test

The DOL rule governs FLSA compliance — minimum wage and overtime obligations. It doesn't govern every relevant classification question. Three separate bodies of law use different tests:

**IRS test for payroll tax purposes.** The IRS uses a "behavioral control / financial control / type of relationship" framework (the "common law" test) to determine whether workers are employees for federal payroll tax purposes. In practice, the IRS test and the DOL economic reality test often reach the same conclusion for home care workers.

**State workers' compensation.** Most states have their own independent contractor definitions for workers' compensation purposes. Some states (Ohio, California) are quite aggressive about requiring coverage for workers who would otherwise be classified as contractors. A home care agency with workers' comp violations for 1099 caregivers faces both back premiums and uninsured-loss exposure.

**State wage and hour laws.** Minimum wage, overtime, and paid leave requirements are enforced by state agencies using state-specific tests. Several states have adopted versions of the ABC test (A: free from control; B: outside usual course of business; C: independently established trade), which is more difficult to satisfy than the federal economic reality test.

A home care operator in Georgia interacts with all three bodies of law simultaneously. Georgia uses a "right to control" test for workers' compensation that shares factors with the federal test but is not identical.

## What correct bookkeeping looks like for each classification

The classification decision has direct accounting consequences that need to be reflected in the books.

**For W-2 employees:**
- Gross wages are a payroll expense
- Employer FICA (Social Security 6.2% on wages up to the Social Security wage base, Medicare 1.45% with no cap) is a separate payroll tax expense — currently 7.65% of gross wages for most workers
- Federal Unemployment Tax (FUTA) — 6.0% on the first $7,000 of wages per employee, with a credit for state unemployment taxes paid, resulting in an effective rate of 0.6% for most employers
- State Unemployment Insurance (SUTA) — rate varies by state and by the agency's claim history; in Georgia the new employer rate is currently 2.7% on the first $9,500 of wages
- Workers' compensation premium, which for home care/personal care workers in Georgia is generally in the range of $8–$12 per $100 of payroll depending on classification code
- Accrued payroll liability for wages earned but not yet paid as of each month-end close

**For 1099 contractors:**
- Contractor payments are an expense when paid (cash basis) or accrued (accrual basis)
- No employer FICA, no FUTA, no SUTA, no workers' comp obligation (at the employer level)
- Form 1099-NEC must be issued annually for payments of $600 or more to each contractor

The difference in total cost — W-2 vs. 1099 — is typically 18 to 25 percent of base wages when all employer-side costs are included. A home care agency paying caregivers $18/hour base and classifying them as employees incurs roughly $21.50–$22.50 per hour in total labor cost. At 1099, the per-payment cost is $18. That gap is real, and it influences pricing, profitability calculations, and distribution decisions.

What the gap does not represent is a free choice the agency makes. If the caregivers should be employees under applicable law, the employer-side costs exist as an obligation whether or not they're being paid. The only thing that changes with misclassification is when the obligation becomes visible — at setup or after an audit.

## How misclassification appears on financial statements

A home care agency that has misclassified W-2 employees as 1099 contractors has, in accounting terms, understated its labor costs and overstated its profits. The employer FICA, SUTA, FUTA, and workers' comp costs that should be accruing as expenses are not appearing on the P&L. The corresponding liability — the amount owed to the IRS, state agencies, and workers' comp carrier — is not appearing on the balance sheet.

If the agency has been operating for three years under this model and is audited in year four, the financial statements for years one through three will need to be reconstructed to reflect the actual labor cost picture. The balance sheet will need to show the accumulated liability, including penalties and interest, as a current or long-term obligation.

For an agency considering a sale, this matters immediately. An acquirer's CPA will review the classification structure and either require reclassification as a condition of the transaction or apply a discount to the purchase price reflecting the contingent liability. Agencies that have been operating on 1099 models for years often discover that their effective EBITDA multiple is significantly lower than they expected once the labor cost structure is correctly accounted for.

## The LOC and distribution interaction

Caregiver classification also affects working capital mechanics in the way described in detail in the [home health agency cash flow article](/articles/home-health-agency-cash-flow-loc-funded-payroll). W-2 payroll — with its employer taxes due on a federal deposit schedule (semi-weekly or monthly depending on prior year tax liability) — creates a more rigid cash timing obligation than 1099 payments. Employer FICA deposits can't be delayed without penalties the way a contractor payment sometimes can.

This means that an agency transitioning from a 1099 model to a W-2 model needs to plan for the change in working capital requirements. The line of credit facility may need to be expanded. The owner's distribution timing may need to change. These are planning decisions that are substantially easier to make proactively than reactively.

## Franchise agreements and the classification question

For franchised home care agencies, classification decisions interact with franchise agreements in ways that aren't always obvious. Some franchise agreements specify staffing model requirements, including whether caregivers are employees or contractors. Franchisees operating on a model that differs from the franchisor's standard may create exposure not only under labor law but under the franchise agreement itself.

Franchise royalty payments — typically a percentage of gross revenue — are calculated on the same revenue base regardless of whether the caregiver labor producing that revenue is correctly classified. A franchise home care agency that discovers it needs to reclassify a large portion of its workforce will see its effective royalty rate as a share of total cost increase, since the denominator (gross revenue) stays the same while the numerator (total costs including now-visible employer-side labor costs) increases.

## The home care owner's responsibility before the audit

The framing that gets home care operators in trouble is the following: "We haven't been audited, so the current structure is fine." This conflates the absence of a penalty with the absence of a liability. A liability exists when the obligation arises, not when it is discovered or assessed.

The practical steps for a home care agency owner concerned about classification:

1. **Review the actual working arrangements** of caregivers currently classified as 1099 against the DOL's six-factor test. Not the job descriptions — the actual arrangements. How are they assigned? Can they turn down assignments without penalty? Can they work for other agencies simultaneously?

2. **Get a tax and legal opinion** on the classification if the arrangements are ambiguous. The cost of an opinion is small relative to the potential liability. The opinion also provides a reasonable-basis defense that can mitigate penalties if the IRS later disagrees.

3. **Use the IRS Voluntary Classification Settlement Program** if the conclusion is that reclassification is appropriate. The VCSP allows businesses to prospectively reclassify workers as employees at a reduced tax cost — 10 percent of the employment tax liability for the most recent tax year — compared to the cost of a full audit.

4. **Update the chart of accounts and accrual methodology** to reflect the correct cost structure going forward. If the agency moves to W-2, the bookkeeping changes on day one.

The DOL's 2024 rule didn't create the obligation to correctly classify caregivers. That obligation exists under the FLSA and IRS regulations regardless of which administration is in office. The 2024 rule changed the weight of certain factors, making it harder to argue that economically dependent workers are independent contractors. What hasn't changed is the underlying standard: is this person in business for themselves, or are they working in your business?

For most non-medical home care agency caregivers, the honest answer to that question is the same under the 2024 rule as it was under any prior test.
